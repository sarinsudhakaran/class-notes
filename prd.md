# Discovery PRD — Shared subscription in residential communities

Nov 27, 2026 · @Sarin

Gated societies share their infrastructure but their households still buy every recurring service alone: one family we interviewed pays outside the gate for daycare and music classes while its clubhouse hall was booked twice in four years. Our first scope-down inside the shared economy is shared subscription — residents collectively accessing professional activities or services, potentially in shared spaces. The evidence for it is thin (2–3 of 21 interviews), and this document says so throughout. This PRD follows the Guru pod Discovery PRD template heading by heading. It stays in the problem space: it records the discovery that led to the shared-subscription scope-down, who hurts and how, and what is still unvalidated. It does not propose a product. Case submission due Nov 28, 2026.

## 0. Scope the Problem Space

**How to read this document.** Every claim carries one of four labels. **Observed** = said by an interviewee or documented in a source we read. **Hypothesis** = our inference, untested. **Suggestion** = an idea raised in a session, recorded but not endorsed. **Unresolved** = a question the group left open. Interview codes (P01–P21, T1–T4) and finding codes (F1–F17) refer to the team's independent evidence review. A template section with no supporting evidence says **Needs research**.

### What exactly are we trying to understand?

Our broad opportunity space is the **shared economy in residential communities**. The first area we are scoping down to is **shared subscription**: residents collectively accessing professional activities or services, potentially using shared community spaces. We want to understand whether a real, recurring problem sits there, for whom, and what keeps it unsolved.

### What is inside our scope?

- Mid-segment to premium gated societies of 150–500 homes in Tier 1 cities — the interview target the group set on 25 Nov.
- Professional activities and services consumed by households on a recurring basis: coaching and classes (swimming, sports, yoga, karate, dance, music), tuition, childcare, fitness training.
- The shared spaces they could use: clubhouse, hall, courts, pool, gym.
- The three parties any such arrangement needs: resident households, providers (the session of 25 Nov filed gym trainers, karate and yoga teachers and tutors under "small businesses"), and the managing committee or facility manager.

### What is outside our scope?

- Solutions, features and apps. The group is still in the observatory phase (Deepak, 26 Nov: "don't jump to solutions").
- The other shareability categories — equipment, gadgets, vehicles, parking, short-let, food, jobs — except as the wider discovery that led here (§7).
- Worker welfare, gate friction and society finances as problems in their own right. They have stronger evidence than our chosen space (§7 says so plainly) and appear here only where they constrain it.
- Non-resident customers, old small co-operative buildings, and pre-handover developer townships.

### Which stakeholders might be involved?

Residents (owners, tenants, non-resident owners), providers of activities and services, domestic staff, the managing committee and office staff, facility management agencies, security, incumbent society apps, and builders before handover. Mapped in §2.

### What are the different directions this problem could go?

- **Who organises:** a resident volunteer, the committee, a facility manager, an outside operator, or nobody (today's default).
- **Who pays:** each household, the society from maintenance, or a mix. **Unresolved** — "the society pays" was raised in discussion and is a hypothesis, not a premise.
- **What is shared:** the provider's time (a batch), the space, the cost, the risk of a no-show, or the effort of finding and vetting.
- **Which service first:** children's activities, adult fitness, tuition, childcare, or the specialised upkeep societies already contract.

### What do we already know?

- **Observed:** one household pays outside the society for daycare and music classes while its clubhouse hall was booked twice in four years (P05).
- **Observed:** asked whether residents had ever bought, hired or arranged something as a group, the same resident answered "na" (P05). The team's consolidated sheet records "never bought anything together; every flat buys, hires and repairs alone".
- **Observed:** one committee split a ₹7,000 a month pool-maintenance contract with the neighbouring society, bringing it to ₹3,500 (P02).
- **Observed:** operators already run coaching inside Indian societies under RWA contracts (§4.4).

### What do we not know yet?

- Whether residents want activities inside the gate at all — no interview asked.
- Whether any committee would organise, permit or fund a shared subscription — we interviewed one former member and no serving one.
- How much shared space is actually used. The evidence review rates "under-used shared assets" **very low** confidence: two or three interview entries, no usage data.

## 1. Problem Space

### Case Summary

**What is the case about?**

India's growing apartment ecosystem, treated as a complex economic and social system. The brief (23 Nov) was to discover unmet needs and emerging opportunities from first principles — explicitly *not* to improve MyGate or NoBrokerHood.

On 24 Nov the group compared an independent house with a society along defined dimensions and reached one takeaway: the residential community, not the household, is emerging as an economic unit.

| Dimension | Independent house | Society |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand | Geographically fragmented | Concentrated |
| Infrastructure | Individually owned | Partially shared |
| Resources | Individually used | Pooled |
| Consumption | Independent | *Potentially* coordinated |
| Information | Fragmented | Shared via community groups |
| Trust | Personal relationships | Community or platform-based |
| Governance | None | RWA / association |
| Labour | Hired individually | Shared across households |

The session's conclusion: infrastructure has become collective, but consumption, commerce and behaviour are still mostly individual. "Wherever you see a mismatch, that is the most compelling place to do discovery." The group's one-line objective became: **map the potential for unrealised collective value in residential societies** — the gap between what collective infrastructure makes possible and what individual behaviour currently captures.

**What are we trying to understand?**

Three questions framed the work (Deepak, 24 Nov): what has fundamentally changed, why that change matters, and why this ecosystem is worth investigating. For the scope-down they become:

1. Do households in a society individually buy professional activities or services that they could access collectively?
2. If so, what stops the collective version from happening — and is that blocker a missing coordination mechanism, a trust gap, or a structural conflict nobody can remove?
3. Whose problem is it most acutely: the household's, the provider's, or the committee's?

We do not yet have an answer to any of the three. This document records how far the evidence goes on each.

## 2. Stakeholder Mapping

The full actor list was compiled on 25 Nov by walking the journey from gate to flat to common areas. Below are the actors a shared subscription would touch; the template's example rows (customer, gig worker, platform, apartment/security, merchant) are mapped onto this ecosystem.

| Stakeholder | Role | Why are they important? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Customer — resident owner** | Pays maintenance, votes at the general body, consumes services | Source of demand; the only group with a vote on rules and society spending |
| **Customer — tenant** | Pays rent (and often maintenance), consumes services, usually cannot vote | About 40–45 of \~90 occupied flats in one interviewed complex (P03); some societies give tenants different gym or pool timings (T2) |
| **Merchant — activity and service provider** (gym trainer, swim or sports coach, yoga, karate, dance or music teacher, tutor) | Delivers the session; classified by the group as a "small business" | The supply side of any shared subscription. **None was interviewed** |
| **Merchant — specialised vendor** (pool, STP/WTP, lift, CCTV on annual contracts) | Keeps shared infrastructure running | The one place the collective already contracts and pays for a professional service (P02, CCTV vendor with 10–12 societies) |
| **Gig / domestic worker** (maid, cook, nanny, app-booked home-service worker) | Serves several households, hired by each separately | Already a service shared informally across flats "but managed by no one"; a possible supply pool for childcare |
| **Apartment — managing committee / association** | Elected, unpaid; sets rules, approves vendors, allots common areas, spends within a cap | The gatekeeper and, for anything collective, the decision-maker. On paper the general body is supreme; in practice the committee decides day to day |
| **Apartment — office or facility manager** | Salaried; collects dues, handles complaints, coordinates vendors | Carries out whatever the committee agrees; in one society everything is manual (P03) |
| **Apartment — security** | Enforces entry rules for every non-resident | Every provider visit passes through the gate; known people are re-approved each time (F1) |
| **Platform** (MyGate, NoBrokerHood, ADDA, JioGate) | Holds resident identity, gate approval, billing, amenity booking | Chosen by the committee, not the resident; already ships class and amenity booking |
| **Builder / developer** | Controls common areas and funds until handover | Decides what amenities exist; out of scope after handover |

**Who decides, who pays, who benefits — today.** The household pays the provider directly. The committee decides whether the provider may use common space. The society receives nothing, or a cut where one is levied. No single party is responsible for the arrangement continuing.

## 3. Current Ecosystem

### Current workflow

How a household gets a recurring professional service today, assembled from interviews about domestic help, repairs and vendors. **We have no first-hand account of a class or coach being hired** — the class-specific steps below come from desk research and are marked.

| Step | What happens | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A need arises | A household wants daycare, a music class, a tennis coach | **Observed** (P05, T2) |
| Asks the group | "There is a WhatsApp group with community members. We can seek contacts/suggestions from everyone" (P05); MyGate lists domestic help "with reviews, attendance, with available free slot" (P04) | **Observed** |
| A provider is found | By neighbour referral — a makeup artist living in the society is found through neighbours (T1). Providers approaching societies directly | Referral **observed**; provider-side approach is a **hypothesis** from the team's persona sheet |
| Committee approves | The committee decides who may use common space and on what terms; one society levies 10% on instructors, another allows trainers freely | **Secondary only** (2 Reddit threads) |
| Gate approval | Fresh approval on every visit; an app-booked worker is refused "even when the guard knows her" and the job is cancelled (T4) | **Observed** for service workers; assumed for coaches |
| Sessions run | Household pays the provider directly | Cash/UPI detail is **secondary only** |

### Current process

There is no process. Nothing in the path is written down: approvals are verbal, payment is direct, and records do not pass between committees — a former committee member restored the society's pool and "handed it over to the next people asking them to preserve it, but they don't maintain it" (P02). The one formal process a society does run is the vendor contract for upkeep: monthly lift servicing (P03), a ₹7,000 a month pool-cleaning contract (P02), CCTV annual maintenance contracts (vendor interview).

### Stakeholder interactions

- **Household ↔ provider:** direct, one to one, per household. Each household finds, vets, schedules and pays separately.
- **Household ↔ household:** almost none on purchases. "Have residents ever bought, hired or arranged something as a group?" — "na" (P05). Residents arrange festivals and pujas together, not services.
- **Committee ↔ provider:** permission and space only; where a society contracts a vendor it is for upkeep, not for services residents consume.
- **Committee ↔ household:** rules, dues and complaints; decisions at the AGM or by poll (P04).
- **Security ↔ provider:** approval at every visit, with discretion.

### Insights

For a third person: households already share *infrastructure* and already share *workers informally*, but they buy every recurring service alone, through personal networks, with nothing recorded. The society contracts professionally only for upkeep. **Hypothesis:** the gap is not discovery (WhatsApp and the app directory cover it) but the absence of anyone who organises a collective purchase — the consolidated sheet's own reading is "nobody organises it; coordination effort too high". How a class is actually arranged inside a society is **Needs research**: we have not spoken to one provider or one serving committee member.

## 4. Secondary Research

Desk research was run by three groups on 24 Nov (economic, behavioural and structural lenses) and extended through 27 Nov. Much of the first pass was AI-assisted; the group's own rule is to treat those numbers as leads and cross-check before relying on them. Source quality is stated wherever a figure is used.

### 4.1 Industry Trends

**What are the major trends in this industry?**

- **Gated living is becoming the urban default.** Redseer estimates 16M gated-community households in the top 50 cities in 2021, projected to 24M in 2026 and 32M by 2031 — from 32% to about half of households in those cities. *Consultancy estimate; projections, not actuals.*
- **Societies are getting bigger.** 1,000+ unit townships are replacing 10–40 unit blocks in new supply; the gated benchmark is 200–250 flats per society (platform statement), about 212 per complex in Bengaluru federation data.
- **The society has become a unit of urban infrastructure.** Sewage treatment, bulk-waste processing, rooftop solar and shared EV charging are now expected or mandated at society level (BWSSB, BBMP, MNRE — government and news sources rated high or good).
- **Platforms sit at the gate.** MyGate reports 25,000+ communities and about 4M households; Redseer puts platform penetration at roughly 25% of gated communities today.
- **Amenities sell flats.** Over 60% of homebuyers prioritise projects with full clubhouse facilities and 64% prefer gated communities. *Surveys of preference, not behaviour.*

**What is changing?**

- Spending is concentrating: those households are about 32% of top-50-city households but about 45% of consumer spending (Redseer, 2021).
- Household chores are increasingly outsourced — cooking, cleaning and childcare hired daily; nuclear families are about 70% of urban households (NFHS-5).
- Regulation is formalising the association: Tamil Nadu (2022) and Karnataka (2026) move to one mandatory association per project with a state regulator; Maharashtra's draft rules let a committee spend up to ₹3 lakh without a general body meeting.
- Private coaching is rising: about 38% of secondary students were in private tutoring in 2025, up from 30% in 2018 (cited in the team's scoring notes from the CMS Education Survey 2025).

**What is driving this change?**

The 24 Nov session reduced it to three first-principles mechanisms: **economics of density** (serve more customers in a smaller area at lower cost), **economics of scale** (aggregated demand buys what no household buys alone), and **social norms and proof** (neighbours' choices shape one's own). It also set a boundary: higher purchasing power comes from moving to the city, not from living in a society — so density, not affluence, is the variable.

**Insights**

For a third person: the trend data shows collective *obligation* growing fast (societies must run utilities) while collective *choice* has not followed. One desk source states the counter-trend bluntly — collective buying in societies "mostly stalls" on kickback fears, staggered purchases and brand loyalty, and "works only for standard or synchronised spend". A recurring class with a fixed timetable is synchronised spend; whether that makes it an exception is a **hypothesis**.

**Insights**

For a third person: every stakeholder already behaves *as if* services were shared — workers stack households, vendors stack societies, residents pool recommendations — but the purchase itself is never shared. Two things recur on the buying side: a wish for the service **without the social obligation**, and reliance on personal networks for trust. On the supply side the recurring failure is **absence**, not quality. What the provider of activities wants is a blank: that column is hypothesis throughout.

### 4.3 Current Solutions

**How is this problem solved today?**

Mostly it is not solved collectively; each household solves it alone.

- **Individually, outside the gate.** Coaching centres, studios, academies, daycare and crèches. One interviewed household pays outside for daycare and music classes (P05).
- **Individually, inside the gate.** Independent yoga, Zumba, dance, skating and personal trainers already teach inside societies; some committees take a cut or restrict them, others allow them freely. *Evidence is thin: two Reddit threads and one provider website.*
- **Collectively, but only for upkeep.** The society contracts pool cleaning, lift servicing, CCTV maintenance and STP operation.

**What products/services already exist?**

- **Society apps.** MyGate, NoBrokerHood and ADDA all ship amenity booking with time slots, capacity, cancellation charges, per-flat caps and tenant rates. NoBrokerHood lets a committee set up coaching classes for residents to book. *Vendor documentation: it shows what is possible, not what societies switch on.*
- **Managed operators.** Firms that staff and run a society's sports or gym programme under an RWA or facility-management contract (§4.4).
- **Adjacent consumer platforms.** Playo for sports booking, Cult.fit for group fitness, Urban Company for one-off home services.

**What workarounds do people use?**

| Who | Workaround | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Residents | Community WhatsApp group for contacts and recommendations | P04, P05 |
| Residents | Neighbour referral for a professional who already lives in the society | T1 |
| Resident | Pays a trainer instead of finding playing partners | T2 |
| Committee | Splits a contract with the neighbouring society to halve the cost | P02 |
| Committee | Raises a one-off levy per household when the reserve is short | P02 |
| Residents and instructors (online) | Under-quote the fee or pay directly to avoid a society's 10% cut; "register the trainer as a guest" | Reddit, 1 thread |
| Home businesses (NCR) | Run parlours, gyms and clinics from flats, drawing complaints to the RWA | ThePrint ground report |

**Insights**

For a third person: the tools to book a shared space already exist and nobody complains about them — 0 of 90 critical App Store reviews across three apps mention amenity booking. Where apps *sell services*, the anger is about the provider: 10 of 30 critical NoBrokerHood reviews concern no-shows, damage or overcharging. So the existing solutions cover space and scheduling; none of them covers the provider turning up, and none of them makes households act together. Whether zero booking complaints means "works fine" or "barely used" cannot be told from these sources.

### 4.6 Observations

Patterns only, no "why". Recurrence is counted in interview entries out of 21 (India), as the team's evidence review counted them.

- Trust, complaints and trade run on WhatsApp, phone and paper; apps handle logging. *8 of 21 entries, 6 roles, 5 interviewers.*
- Non-residents lose time being approved at the gate, including people the guard knows. *8 of 21.*
- Workers serve several households or societies from one place — guards on double shifts, a car cleaner employing others, a CCTV vendor with 10–12 societies. *6 of 21.*
- Households change scope and timing of work without extra pay. *4 of 21.*
- Households compete for the same helpers and lose them to slightly better offers. *4 of 21.*
- Governance rests on a few people; records and upkeep do not survive a committee change. *4 of 21.*
- Neighbour ties are selective: weak ties in 4 of 21 entries, strong in 2 of 21, and the strong ones form through children.
- Residents buy services through personal recommendation, not ratings.
- Shared amenities are under-used while similar services are bought outside. *2–3 entries; the evidence review rates this very low confidence.*
- Residents have not bought, hired or arranged a service together. *1 direct answer (P05); recorded as a pattern in the consolidated sheet.*
- A committee shares a fixed cost across a society boundary when the saving is visible. *1 entry (P02).*
- Online, where a society app sells a service, complaints are about the provider not the booking; and where a society taxes an instructor, people discuss going around it.
- In Gurgaon reviews the amenity complaint is uptime, not absence — a pool "not working about 90% of the time" at one society, a club handed over years late at another.

### 4.8 Research Gaps

**What questions are still unanswered?**

- How much are clubhouses, halls, courts and pools actually used? Under-use and low demand look identical without this.
- What do instructors earn inside societies, how do classes fill, and what cut do societies take?
- Why do committees set the rules they do on tenants, caps and instructor cuts?
- How often are the class and paid-amenity settings in society apps switched on?

**What assumptions are we making?**

- That a household buying a service outside would prefer it inside.
- That vendor-style contracting for upkeep would extend to services residents consume.
- That online complaint volume says something about prevalence. *The Reddit sample was gathered with complaint-loaded searches; counts are ceilings, not rates.*

**What can only be answered through primary research?**

- Whether residents want more activities inside the gate — no source asks.
- A serving committee member's actual reasoning and fears.
- A provider's side of the arrangement, start to finish.

## 5. Key Insights

The secondary research in five lines. The "because" clauses are our reading and remain hypotheses.

1. **We observed** that societies are now required to act collectively on utilities — sewage, waste, solar, EV charging — **because** regulation and missing city services push those jobs onto them, **which means** the society is already a functioning collective buyer, but only where it is forced to be.
2. **We observed** that households still buy every recurring service alone, through personal networks, **because** nobody organises a joint purchase and the coordination effort falls on whoever volunteers, **which means** the unrealised value is in coordination, not in infrastructure or discovery.
3. **We observed** that society apps already offer amenity and class booking and draw no complaints about it, while the services sold through them draw complaints about providers not turning up, **because** software can schedule a slot but cannot make a person arrive, **which means** the unsolved part of shared access is reliability of the provider, not booking.
4. **We observed** residents choosing paid, low-obligation arrangements over neighbourly ones, **because** closeness inside a society is hard to step back from, **which means** a shared subscription would have to work without requiring neighbours to become friends.
5. **We observed** a committee splitting a pool contract with the society next door, yet no instance of residents jointly buying a service, **because** a fixed upkeep cost is easy to divide and carries no personal preference, **which means** collective purchase is proven for standard spend and unproven for anything a household chooses for itself.

**One interesting thing we discovered is** that the evidence is strongest where our chosen space is weakest. Gate friction, WhatsApp-over-apps and worker shift-stacking each recur in 6–8 of 21 interviews; under-used shared assets rests on 2–3. We scoped down to a space the data only hints at.

## 7. Scope Down

**Which direction?** Shared subscription: residents collectively accessing professional activities or services, potentially using shared community spaces.

The seven consolidated shareability categories were scored out of 15 on interview evidence, outside evidence, frequency, money already spent, and ease and trust.

&#91;embedded content: team scoring workbook, Opportunity Space activity · 7 categories, 5 factors, max 15\]

### Why this?

- **It is the mismatch the group set out to find.** Infrastructure is collective, consumption individual; this is the most literal case of it.
- **The spend recurs.** Childcare and tuition scored highest (13/15) because payments are weekly and already being made outside the gate. Most other categories are occasional transactions.
- **The asset already exists.** No one has to buy, insure or lend anything; the space is built and funded through maintenance.
- **It does not depend on neighbours becoming friends.** Several residents avoid ties on purpose; a paid, professional arrangement fits that better than lending or carpooling.
- **A committee has already done a version of it.** The shared pool contract is the one observed instance of collective purchase across households.

### Why not the others?

| Direction | Why not now |
| --- | --- |
| Internal labour market for household help (S6, highest group score at 25) | Crowded — NoBrokerHood claims 9 in 10 househelps in gated communities are found through its app; and the tension runs both ways (bidding up versus committee wage caps) |
| Worker welfare and site conditions (S4, 19) | Strong need, but Deepak's challenge stands: no clear business return for vendors or societies, and the status quo benefits everyone except the worker |
| Gate friction (S1, 15) | Best evidenced, but low economic value in the vote, and pre-approval is partly solved by MyGate |
| Food (12/15) | Strongest interview evidence in shareability, but food-safety liability and heavy competition from Swiggy, Zomato and quick commerce |
| Jobs and referrals (10/15) | Infrequent per person; crowded |
| Vehicles, parking, short-let (8/15) | No interview raised it; bye-laws on parking and short stays; carpooling incumbents exist and the session noted Uber Pool's failure and off-app drift |
| Lending and renting things (7/15) | Small, rare deals; buy-and-sell already exists in both major apps |

### What evidence supports this?

- **Observed:** daycare and music classes paid for outside; hall booked twice in four years (P05).
- **Observed:** facilities used "only when family visits"; a resident who would pay a tennis trainer (T2).
- **Observed:** a tenant who pays for amenities he does not use (Godrej Royal Woods).
- **Observed:** pool contract shared between two societies (P02).
- **Secondary:** operators already sell coaching to societies; apps already ship class booking.

### Weak links in this reasoning

These are stated so the next reader can challenge the choice rather than inherit it.

- **The chosen space has the weakest evidence of the ten.** The evidence review rates under-used shared assets very low: 2–3 interview entries. Three other spaces have 6–8.
- **The top score rests on one interview.** The scoring note itself says the strongest support for childcare comes from a single resident with a child, and asks for 2–3 more parents and a resident tutor before presenting it as the top pick.
- **The bundle flatters the choice.** Shared subscription spans one category at 13 and two at 9. The same note says hobbies and sports is something "people won't pay much for".
- **The scores are one person's judgement**, and the note invites the team to re-weight them.
- **No one was asked.** No interview asked residents whether they want activities inside the society, and the one direct question about buying together got "na".
- **Direct counter-evidence exists.** A desk source says collective buying in societies "mostly stalls"; Gurgaon reviews show amenities that exist but do not run.
- **We did not find consensus recorded.** The 26 Nov notes show shareability drawing the highest votes; the narrowing to shared subscription is not documented in any transcript in the folder. **Unresolved** — the team should confirm how and when that call was made.
- **Deepak's guidance on 26 Nov was to zoom out,** not lock onto a persona or solution early. Scoping down this far is in tension with that and should be treated as provisional.

- Reliance on WhatsApp and personal recommendation, across residents, a tenant, businesses and staff.

### What surprised us?

- A resident who says there is "no bonding" and asks for "more bonding", next to a resident who avoids neighbours on purpose. Weak ties are sometimes a cost and sometimes a preference.
- Paying as a way to avoid a relationship, rather than money being the awkward part.
- A committee buying across the society boundary — the pool contract shared with the next-door complex.
- A tenant who calls unused amenities good value: "You couldn't get all of that commercially at this price point."
- The loudest problems online (rules, charges) were milder in interviews, and the most severe in interviews (worker conditions) barely appear online.

### Which assumptions were wrong?

| We assumed | What the round showed |
| --- | --- |
| Living close together creates community | Most residents know few neighbours; ties are selective |
| Society apps are how residents find and manage services | Apps log; people decide through WhatsApp and referral |
| Unused amenities mean residents feel short-changed | At least one tenant sees them as value for money regardless |

## 14. Final Problem Statement

**Status: draft hypothesis, not final.** The template places this after primary research on the chosen direction. That research has not happened yet, and the 26 Nov session noted the problem statement "is not yet clear". What follows is the statement we would take into the next round to test.

**Who?** Households in mid-segment to premium gated societies of 150–500 homes in Tier 1 cities — first, those with children aged about 3–12. *Persona not validated.*

**Problem?** Each household separately finds, vets and pays for recurring professional activities and services — often outside the gate — while the society's shared spaces sit lightly used. Residents have a collective asset and no collective way to use it for the services they already buy.

**Why?** *Hypotheses, in the order we would test them:*

1. Nobody organises it: coordinating neighbours takes effort and falls on a volunteer.
2. Residents prefer arrangements that carry no social obligation, and shared ones look like they do.
3. The committee controls the space, is overloaded, and gains nothing from saying yes.
4. A shared arrangement depends on one provider turning up, and nobody is accountable when they do not.

**Evidence?**

- **For:** daycare and music classes bought outside with the hall used twice in four years (P05); facilities used "only when family visits" (T2); "na" to ever having arranged something as a group (P05); a pool contract shared between two societies (P02); operators already selling coaching to societies.
- **Against or missing:** the theme appears in 2–3 of 21 interviews and was never asked about directly; no usage data; a tenant who is content paying for amenities he does not use; a desk source saying collective buying mostly stalls; no provider and no serving committee member interviewed.

**What would change this statement.** Parents telling us the outside option is preferred, not merely tolerated; booking records showing spaces are in fact used; or committees who have tried this and stopped.
